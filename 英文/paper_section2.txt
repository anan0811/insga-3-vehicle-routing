2. Literature Review
================================================================================

This section reviews research directly relevant to the problem formulation and
solution methodology of this paper. We organize the review into four themes:
(2.1) the evolution of two-echelon vehicle routing problems and their extensions;
(2.2) green vehicle routing and carbon emission modeling; (2.3) multi-objective
evolutionary algorithms applied to VRP; and (2.4) local search and hybrid
metaheuristics. The review concludes by identifying the research gap that
motivates this work.


2.1 Two-Echelon Vehicle Routing Problems and Extensions
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP), introduced by Dantzig and Ramser [1], is
among the most extensively studied combinatorial optimization problems in
operations research. In urban logistics, the classical single-echelon VRP fails
to capture the hierarchical structure of modern distribution networks, where
freight moves from central warehouses through intermediate consolidation points
before reaching end customers. This observation gave rise to the Two-Echelon
VRP (2E-VRP), in which large first-echelon (FE) vehicles transport consolidated
loads from depots to intermediate facilities called satellites, and smaller
second-echelon (SE) vehicles complete the last-mile delivery to customers.

The foundational mathematical formulations of 2E-VRP were established by
Perboli et al. [3] and Crainic et al. [4], who proposed exact branch-and-cut
methods applicable to small-scale instances. Subsequent surveys, notably Sluijk
et al. [2], systematically categorized 2E-VRP variants and demonstrated the
broad applicability of this two-tier structure across freight consolidation,
parcel delivery, and humanitarian logistics. A key challenge identified in these
surveys is the combinatorial explosion arising from the interdependence between
echelon-level decisions: the choice of which customers to assign to which
satellite directly determines the demand profile that FE vehicles must serve,
creating a tightly coupled bi-level optimization structure.

A practically important extension is the integration of simultaneous pickup and
delivery (SPD), in which SE vehicles both deliver goods to and collect goods
from customers on the same route. The 2E-CVRP-SPD formulation captures the
operational reality of reverse logistics, where product returns and pickups occur
alongside standard deliveries. Belgin et al. [7] proposed one of the first
exact models for 2E-CVRP-SPD and demonstrated the difficulty of the problem
even for moderate instance sizes. Zhou et al. [8] extended this framework by
incorporating time window constraints, showing that SPD feasibility under
capacity constraints requires careful load tracking across each route segment.
Li et al. [10] further investigated 2E-VRP-SPD with stochastic demand, using
a robust optimization approach to handle demand uncertainty in the SE layer.
Collectively, these works establish that the SPD constraint introduces a
fundamentally different load evolution compared to delivery-only VRP, requiring
explicit modeling of cumulative load at each node to verify feasibility.

The multi-depot extension of 2E-VRP (MD-2E-VRP) recognizes that real logistics
networks often feature multiple warehouses or distribution centers serving
overlapping geographic areas. Kancharla and Ramadurai [17] formulated a
multi-depot two-echelon problem for urban freight and showed that coordinated
satellite selection significantly reduces total transportation cost compared to
single-depot solutions. Zhou et al. [18] addressed the MD-2E-VRP with time
windows using a hybrid genetic algorithm, demonstrating that the added depot-
satellite assignment decisions substantially increase solution complexity.
Studies on collaborative multi-depot routing [44] further highlight that
allowing vehicles from different depots to share satellites can yield cost
reductions of 5–15% relative to independently operated depot systems.

The multi-period dimension extends VRP planning horizons from a single day to
multiple days, enabling the optimization of customer frequency and vehicle
utilization across time. Mourgaya and Vanderbeck [21] introduced a column
generation approach for multi-period VRP that partitioned customers into groups
served on different days, reducing per-day route length while maintaining service
frequency requirements. Wen et al. [22] developed a multi-period two-echelon
model for city logistics and showed that multi-day coordination at the satellite
level allows more consistent FE vehicle load utilization. Incorporating multiple
service periods within each day—as in our formulation—further increases the
degree of freedom by allowing the system to shift demand between morning and
afternoon slots when route congestion or capacity issues arise.

The combination of multi-depot, multi-day, multi-period, and SPD constraints
into a single unified problem formulation is relatively rare in the literature.
Wang et al. [32] addressed a multi-depot multi-period 2E-VRP for cold-chain
logistics, coupling temperature control constraints with period-level planning,
while Wang et al. [36] extended this to include collaborative satellite sharing
across planning periods. However, to our knowledge, no prior work simultaneously
addresses all five characteristics — two-echelon structure, multiple depots,
multi-day multi-period horizon, SPD, and three-objective optimization — as
captured in the 2E-MD-MD-MP-CVRP-SPD formulation of this study.


2.2 Green Vehicle Routing and Carbon Emission Modeling
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The transportation sector accounts for approximately 23% of global CO₂
emissions (IEA, 2022), and logistics operations constitute a substantial share
of this total. The growing pressure from environmental regulations, carbon
taxation, and corporate sustainability commitments has elevated green vehicle
routing — which explicitly incorporates emission objectives into the route
optimization framework — to a mainstream research agenda.

Early green VRP models relied on simple fuel consumption proxies, typically
treating emissions as proportional to distance traveled. More sophisticated
emission models account for the nonlinear dependence of fuel consumption and
emissions on vehicle speed and payload. The MEET model (Methodologies for
Estimating Air Pollutant Emissions from Transport) [26], developed by the
European Commission, provides a comprehensive multi-component emission
estimation framework widely adopted in logistics research. The MEET model
decomposes vehicle emissions into three speed-dependent components: the base
emission rate function φ(v), the load correction function ψ(v), and the road
slope correction function r(v). For a 3.5-ton box truck — the representative
vehicle class for urban last-mile delivery — the MEET model yields polynomial
relationships in vehicle speed, allowing efficient computation of per-arc
emissions given route speed and load profile [26]. Li et al. [43] applied
the MEET model to a multi-objective green VRP with time windows (MOGVRPTW),
demonstrating that MEET-based emission objectives produce qualitatively
different Pareto frontiers compared to simple distance-based proxies, because
the load correction factor creates interactions between vehicle load and emission
rate that pure distance models cannot capture.

Green two-echelon VRP research has grown substantially in recent years. Jepsen
et al. [45] introduced a green 2E-VRP model incorporating satellite opening
costs and emission constraints for city freight, demonstrating that the choice
of satellite locations significantly influences the emission profile of both
echelons. Studies on hybrid-fleet 2E-VRP [46] have shown that substituting
conventional diesel vehicles with electric vehicles at the SE layer can reduce
urban emissions by 30–60%, though battery capacity constraints introduce
additional routing complexity. For the SE layer operating in urban environments,
fixed travel speed assumptions (as in our formulation) are common and empirically
justified: Li et al. [43] adopted a fixed SE speed of 50 km/h based on urban
delivery practices, which collapses the MEET emission function to a load-
dependent constant per unit distance. This simplification maintains emission
sensitivity to vehicle load while avoiding the need to model speed profiles on
individual arcs.

Multi-objective green VRP formulations typically include transportation cost and
emission minimization as competing objectives, since aggressive emission
reduction often requires route detours or additional vehicles that raise cost.
Several studies have demonstrated that Pareto-optimal trade-off frontiers between
these two objectives are non-trivial and contain solutions qualitatively superior
to single-objective optima [45, 47]. Including vehicle count as an additional
minimization objective — as in our three-objective formulation — further enriches
the decision space, since reducing the fleet size simultaneously lowers fixed
vehicle costs and can improve per-vehicle load utilization, though it may
increase per-vehicle route length and thus per-vehicle emissions.


2.3 Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms for VRP
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Multi-objective evolutionary algorithms (MOEAs) are the dominant solution
paradigm for VRP problems with multiple competing objectives, owing to their
ability to maintain a diverse population of non-dominated solutions and
approximate the Pareto front in a single optimization run. NSGA-II (Non-
dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II), proposed by Deb et al. [42], introduced
fast non-dominated sorting and crowding-distance-based selection and became the
reference MOEA for VRP applications throughout the 2000s and 2010s. NSGA-II
has been successfully applied to variants of green VRP [47], SPD-VRP [7], and
multi-depot VRP [17], demonstrating its broad applicability. However, NSGA-II's
crowding distance mechanism degrades in performance as the number of objectives
exceeds two, because Euclidean crowding distance becomes a poor proxy for
diversity in high-dimensional objective spaces.

NSGA-III, proposed by Deb and Jain [41], addresses this limitation by replacing
crowding distance with structured reference points distributed uniformly on a
normalized hyperplane in objective space. The reference point mechanism
explicitly controls diversity across the full extent of the Pareto front and
scales gracefully to three or more objectives, making NSGA-III the algorithm
of choice for many-objective optimization problems. For our three-objective
formulation (cost, vehicle count, emissions), NSGA-III provides a principled
mechanism to ensure that the returned solution set covers cost-vehicle trade-
offs, cost-emission trade-offs, and balanced three-way compromise solutions
simultaneously. Empirical comparisons on multi-objective VRP benchmarks have
consistently shown that NSGA-III produces broader and more uniformly distributed
Pareto fronts than NSGA-II when three or more objectives are present [43].

Despite its theoretical advantages, standard NSGA-III exhibits well-documented
weaknesses when applied to vehicle routing problems. First, random initialization
produces low-quality initial solutions that require many generations to repair,
slowing convergence. Second, standard crossover and mutation operators (such as
OBX or swap mutation) operate at the chromosome level without exploiting the
geometric structure of routes, limiting the speed of local improvement. Third,
NSGA-III lacks any explicit mechanism to escape local optima once the population
converges to a suboptimal region of the Pareto front. These weaknesses have
motivated a large body of research on improved NSGA-III variants across diverse
application domains.

Recent NSGA-III enhancements in the literature include transfer learning-based
initialization (which seeds the population with solutions adapted from related
problem instances) [48], centroid-distance reference point adaptation [48],
deep Q-learning guided operator selection [49], co-evolutionary strategies with
reinforcement learning [50], and adaptive penalty-function mechanisms for
handling constraints [51]. These improvements share a common motivation:
augmenting the standard NSGA-III framework with domain-specific knowledge or
adaptive mechanisms to accelerate convergence without sacrificing Pareto front
coverage. In the VRP domain, hybrid NSGA-III approaches that embed local search
within the evolutionary loop have proven particularly effective.

Li et al. [43] proposed the INSGA-III (Improved NSGA-III) for a multi-objective
green VRP with time windows (MOGVRPTW), incorporating nearest-neighbor
initialization and a two-opt local search applied selectively to elite individuals.
Applied to the Solomon benchmark instances with 25–100 customers, INSGA-III
achieved average cost reductions of 39.4%, 53.1%, 75.3%, and 74.1% relative
to NSGA-III, NSGA-II, SPEA2, and MOPSO, respectively, while maintaining
superior Pareto front hypervolume across all three objectives. The work of Li
et al. [43] provides an important point of departure for our study: the INSGA-III
framework demonstrates the value of NN initialization and embedded local search
for VRP, but is limited to a single-depot, single-echelon, single-day setting
without SPD or multi-period structure. Our HNSGA-III extends this direction to
the substantially more complex 2E-MD-MD-MP-CVRP-SPD problem by incorporating
hybrid mutation with a stagnation-triggered VNS, applying 2-opt to all offspring
(not just elites), and coupling the SE evolutionary optimizer with an exact FE
MILP solver via Gurobi.

Other relevant NSGA-III applications to VRP-related problems include multi-
objective scheduling under dynamic constraints [52], flexible job shop routing
with deep reinforcement learning [49], and resource scheduling for distributed
systems [53]. While these applications differ in problem structure, they confirm
the broad utility of NSGA-III as a competitive baseline for complex combinatorial
multi-objective optimization, and highlight the consistent benefit of embedding
problem-specific local improvement operators within the NSGA-III selection loop.


2.4 Local Search and Hybrid Metaheuristics for VRP
────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Local search operators play a central role in high-performance VRP metaheuristics,
providing rapid route-level improvement that genetic operators alone cannot match.
The 2-opt operator, introduced by Lin [54] for the Traveling Salesman Problem
(TSP), reverses a segment of a route to eliminate crossing arcs and remains one
of the most computationally efficient route improvement procedures available.
In VRP contexts, 2-opt is applied within individual vehicle routes to reduce
route length without violating capacity constraints. Studies have shown that
embedding 2-opt within a genetic algorithm's generational cycle — rather than
applying it only as a post-processing step — consistently improves solution
quality, as each newly generated offspring undergoes at least one round of local
refinement before competing in selection [43, 47].

The Or-opt family of operators [55], including relocate (removing a customer
and reinserting it at a better position) and exchange (swapping two customers
between positions), extends 2-opt by allowing non-reversal moves. Relocate is
particularly effective at breaking poor subsequences trapped in local optima of
the 2-opt neighborhood, since it can move a customer across large route segments
in a single step. The exchange operator provides fine-grained local adjustment
at the cost of limited structural change. The combination of exchange and relocate
in a Variable Neighborhood Search (VNS) framework [56] has proven effective for
escaping 2-opt local optima: by alternating between neighborhoods of increasing
size or type, VNS prevents premature convergence while maintaining computational
tractability.

VNS has been applied successfully to a broad range of VRP variants, including
the periodic VRP [21], the multi-depot VRP [17], and the two-echelon VRP [4].
In the hybrid metaheuristic context, VNS is typically triggered reactively —
either when the incumbent solution has not improved for a specified number of
iterations (stagnation-triggered VNS) or when the population diversity drops
below a threshold [56]. The stagnation-triggered approach is computationally
efficient, as it avoids the overhead of full VNS perturbation in generations
where the genetic operators are still making progress. This design principle
directly motivates the HNSGA-III stagnation counter (τ = 5 generations) used
in this study.

Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search (ALNS), proposed by Ropke and Pisinger [57],
provides a more general perturbation framework in which multiple destroy-and-
repair operators are adaptively selected based on their recent historical
performance. While ALNS has demonstrated strong results on benchmark 2E-VRP
instances [4], its operator selection overhead and the need to tune adaptive
weight parameters add implementation complexity relative to the targeted
exchange-relocate VNS used in this study.

The k-means clustering heuristic has been widely used for customer-satellite
assignment in multi-satellite routing problems, providing a fast geometric
partitioning that concentrates customers with similar geographic centroids at
the same satellite. This reduces inter-satellite travel in the SE layer and
provides a natural initialization for satellite-level sub-populations in
population-based algorithms. Our formulation uses k-means clustering as the
preprocessing step that partitions customers to satellites before HNSGA-III
begins its generational evolution, consistent with approaches reported for
multi-satellite 2E-VRP [32, 36].


2.5 Research Gap and Positioning
──────────────────────────────────

The preceding review reveals a clear research gap: while extensive literature
exists on individual extensions of 2E-VRP (SPD, multi-depot, multi-period,
green objectives, and MOEA-based solution methods), no prior work simultaneously
addresses all of the following characteristics in a unified formulation:

  (1) A two-echelon structure with multiple depots and multiple satellites;
  (2) A multi-day, multi-period planning horizon with period-dependent demand;
  (3) Simultaneous pickup and delivery at the second echelon;
  (4) Three competing objectives: total cost, vehicle fleet size, and carbon
      emissions via the MEET model;
  (5) A hybrid MOEA that embeds NN initialization, hybrid mutation, 2-opt on
      all offspring, and stagnation-triggered VNS, coupled with an exact FE
      solver.

The 2E-MD-MD-MP-CVRP-SPD problem defined in this study closes this gap by
integrating all five dimensions into a tractable multi-objective optimization
framework. The HNSGA-III algorithm proposed to solve it synthesizes proven
techniques — NSGA-III reference-point selection, nearest-neighbor initialization,
embedded 2-opt, and stagnation-triggered VNS — into a coherent algorithm design
that directly addresses the four weaknesses of standard NSGA-III identified in
Section 1.3. The two-tier solution architecture, in which HNSGA-III handles the
computationally intensive SE routing and Gurobi MILP solves the FE routing
exactly, further distinguishes this work from purely heuristic approaches and
enables rigorous per-instance FE optimality verification.
